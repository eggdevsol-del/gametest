@lesson 1.1 | Asking so people say yes | 🙏 | stage
hook: You want a later bedtime on Friday. How do you ask?
learn: Good requests are specific, give a reason, and offer something.
learn: "Could I stay up till 9 on Friday to watch the game? I'll be ready on time Saturday."
play: chat | Asking for a later bedtime | them: What's up? | me: Could I stay up till 9 on Friday for the game? I'll still get up on time Saturday*/I want to stay up/Everyone else gets to | them: Hmm, what if you're tired? | me: If I'm grumpy Saturday, I'll go back to 8:30 next week*/I won't be/That's not fair
check: mcq | What makes a request stronger? | A clear ask with a reason* | Whining | Asking five times
check: tf | Offering something in return can help. | true
check: mcq | Which is the clearest request? | Can I have 30 minutes of screen time after homework?* | Can I have screens? | I want stuff
home: Make a clear request at home with a reason and an offer.
skill: asking

@lesson 2.1 | Know your audience | 👥 | stage

learn: Think about what they care about. A parent cares about sleep and school. A friend cares about fun.
learn: Choose reasons that matter to them, not just to you.

@lesson 2.2 | Claim, reason, example | 🧱 | stage
hook: "Can we have a class pet? Please, please, please!" Why might that not work?
learn: Start with what you want. That's your claim.
learn: Give a reason why, then back it up with an example or a fact.
learn: "We should have a class pet. It teaches responsibility. Room 6 has a fish, and they take turns feeding it."
play: build | Build a persuasive case for a longer lunch break | Claim: We should have a longer lunch break; Reason: Kids need time to eat and play; Example: Lots of kids leave food because they rush off to play; Ending: A longer lunch means better eating and better focus
check: mcq | Which is an example that backs up a reason? | Room 6 has a fish, and they take turns feeding it* | Please, please, please! | Because I said so
check: tf | Saying please lots of times is a strong reason. | false | Reasons and examples work better.
check: mcq | What comes first? | The claim, saying what you want* | The example | Saying goodbye
home: Write a claim, a reason and an example for something you'd like to change at home.
skill: persuasion

check: tf | Getting cross is a good way to answer an objection. | false | Listen, then answer with a plan.

@lesson 2.6 | Fair or sneaky? | ⚖️ | online
hook: Is it OK to nag, guilt-trip or fib to get a yes?
learn: Fair persuasion uses honest reasons and respects the other person's choice.
learn: Sneaky tricks include nagging, lying, guilt trips and "everyone else is allowed".
learn: If they still say no, accept it. You can ask again another time with new reasons.
play: scam | Fair persuasion or a sneaky trick? | Here are three reasons, and I've made a plan=real; If you loved me, you'd say yes=scam; Everyone else is allowed!=scam; Could we try it for one week and see?=real; Asking 50 times until they give in=scam | Guilt trips, nagging and "everyone else" are tricks, not reasons.
check: mcq | Which is fair persuasion? | Offering to try it for a week* | If you loved me, you'd say yes | Asking 50 times
check: tf | It's OK to fib if it gets you a yes. | false
check: mcq | They say no after you asked fairly. What now? | Accept it, and maybe ask again later with new reasons* | Sulk for a week | Nag until they give in
home: Spot a sneaky persuasion trick in an ad or a show this week.
skill: persuasion

@lesson 4.4 | Eyes and body | 👀 | stage
