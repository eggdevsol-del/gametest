@lesson 1.1 | The one-page plan | 🗒️ | workshop
hook: You've got a business idea. What should you write down before spending a cent?
learn: A one-page plan covers the problem, your solution, your customers, costs, price and how you'll reach people.
learn: Writing it down shows the gaps before they cost you money.

check: mcq | Who are "customers" in a plan? | The people who'll pay* | Your friends | Your competitors

@lesson 1.2 | Margins | 📐 | stall
hook: Two businesses each sell $1,000 of stuff. Why does one keep $600 and the other $100?
learn: Margin is the share of the price you keep after the cost of making it.
learn: Margin % = (price − cost) ÷ price × 100.
try: Price $10, cost $6. Margin = $4, or 40% :: gap | Price $20, cost $15. Margin = _% | 25
play: price | Which supplier gives you cheaper stock per candle? | Supplier A:48:24:candles; Supplier B:30:12:candles
check: gap | Price $50, cost $30. Margin = _% | 40
check: tf | A bigger margin means you keep more of each sale. | true
check: mcq | What lowers your margin? | Higher costs* | Higher prices | More customers
home: Find a product and estimate its margin.
skill: profit

@lesson 1.5 | Cash flow | 🌊 | bank

check: mcq | What is cash flow? | When money comes in and goes out* | Your logo | Total profit only

@lesson 2.1 | Markup or margin? | 📐 | stall
hook: You buy a candle for $6 and sell it for $10. Is that 40% or 67%?
learn: Markup compares profit to cost. $4 profit on a $6 cost is about a 67% markup.
learn: Margin compares profit to price. $4 profit on a $10 price is a 40% margin.
learn: Same sale, two numbers. Always say which one you mean.
try: Cost $5, price $10. Markup = $5 ÷ $5 = 100% :: gap | Cost $8, price $10. Markup = _% | 25
play: match | Match each sale to its markup | Cost $5, price $10=100%; Cost $10, price $15=50%; Cost $4, price $5=25%; Cost $2, price $8=300%
check: gap | Cost $20, price $25. Markup = _% | 25
check: mcq | Cost $6, price $10. What's the margin? | 40%* | 67% | 60%
check: tf | Markup and margin are always the same number. | false | Markup divides by cost. Margin divides by price.
home: Find something you could resell. Work out its markup and margin.
skill: profit

@lesson 2.2 | Cost-plus pricing | ➕ | workshop
hook: How do many small businesses set prices? They start with cost.
learn: Cost-plus pricing means adding a set markup to what each item costs you.
learn: Count every cost: materials, packaging, platform fees and shipping.
learn: Then check the result against what customers will actually pay.
try: Cost $4 + 50% markup = $6 :: gap | Cost $12 + 50% markup = $_ | 18
play: slider | Earrings: $3.20 materials, $0.80 packaging and a $1 platform fee. With a 100% markup, what's the price? | 0 | 20 | 10 | $ | 0.5 | 0.5
check: gap | Materials $7, packaging $1, fees $2. Total cost = $_ | 10
check: mcq | What's the risk of forgetting a cost? | Your profit is smaller than you think* | Customers pay more | Nothing
check: tf | A cost-plus price should still be checked against what customers will pay. | true
home: List every cost for something you could sell, including the small ones.
skill: pricing

@lesson 2.3 | Value-based pricing | 💎 | stage
hook: A custom-painted phone case costs $8 to make. People happily pay $45. Why?
learn: Value-based pricing sets the price by what the product is worth to the customer.
learn: Unique, handmade or personalised products can often charge well above cost-plus.
learn: Research what similar things sell for, and be clear about what makes yours special.
play: debate | Should you charge $45 for a phone case that costs $8 to make? | Yes, if customers value it at that and you're honest about what it is* | No. You should only ever charge cost plus a little | Some people feel big markups are unfair. Being open about what makes it special lets customers decide.
check: mcq | What does value-based pricing focus on? | What the product is worth to customers* | Only your costs | The cheapest competitor
check: tf | Personalised products can often charge more. | true
check: mcq | A good way to test a value price? | See what similar products sell for* | Guess randomly | Only ask your family
home: Find a handmade product online and estimate what it costs to make.
skill: pricing

@lesson 2.6 | Competitor pricing | 🔍 | online
hook: Three stalls sell candles for $12, $15 and $18. What should yours be?
learn: Check what competitors charge for similar products.
learn: You don't have to be cheapest. Be clear about why yours is worth it.

Yours are $14. | Explain what makes yours better, like longer burn time and natural wax => good: Customers who value quality stay, and you keep your margin. | Cut your price to $7 => bad: Now you barely cover costs, and they might cut again. | Post nasty comments about their candles => bad: Unfair, and it damages your own reputation.
check: mcq | What's a price war? | Sellers keep cutting prices until nobody profits* | A sale on war films | A fair deal
check: tf | You always have to be the cheapest to win customers. | false
check: mcq | Why check competitors' prices? | To see where your price fits* | To copy their logo | To report them
