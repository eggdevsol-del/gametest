@lesson 1.1 | Writing an email | 📧 | online
hook: You email a coach about missing training: "hey cant come". Will that land well?
learn: Good emails have a clear subject, a greeting, a short message, and a sign-off.
learn: Say what you need and by when.
play: order | Build the email in order | Subject: Missing training on Thursday; Hi Coach Sam,; I have a dentist appointment and can't make training on Thursday.; I'll do the drills at home and see you Saturday.; Thanks, Ava

check: tf | A greeting and sign-off make emails more professional. | true
check: mcq | Best way to end an email to a coach? | Thanks, Ava* | bye lol | Nothing
home: Write a practice email to a teacher asking a question.

@lesson 1.3 | Spotting bias | 🔎 | online
hook: Two articles about the same event say opposite things. Who's right?
learn: Bias is leaning one way. Check who wrote it, who paid, and what evidence they use.
learn: Compare several reliable sources.
play: sort | Fact, Opinion | The concert started at 7pm=Fact; The concert was amazing=Opinion; 2,000 people attended=Fact; The band is the best ever=Opinion; Tickets cost $40=Fact; It was worth every cent=Opinion | Fact or opinion?
check: mcq | How can you check a claim? | Compare reliable sources* | Believe the first post | Ask the author only
check: tf | Opinions can be useful but aren't the same as facts. | true
check: mcq | What's a warning sign of bias? | Only one side is shown* | Facts with sources | Dates and names
home: Find one fact and one opinion in a news article.
skill: critical

@lesson 2.1 | Interests, not positions | 🍊 | stage
hook: Two siblings fight over one orange, so they cut it in half. One eats the fruit and bins the peel. The other only wanted the peel for baking. What went wrong?
learn: A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why they want it.
learn: When you ask "why?", you often find a deal where both sides get more.
learn: Here, one could have had all the fruit and the other all the peel.
play: match | Match each position to the interest behind it | I want the car on Saturday=I need to get to my netball game; I want a later curfew=I don't want to be first to leave the party; I want $30 for this job=It takes me two hours; I want the front seat=I get carsick in the back
check: mcq | What's an interest? | The reason behind what someone wants* | A bank's interest rate | A hobby
check: tf | Asking "why?" can uncover a better deal. | true
check: mcq | In the orange story, what was the best deal? | One takes the fruit, the other takes the peel* | Cut it in half | Throw it away
home: Next time you disagree with someone, ask, "Why does that matter to you?"
skill: negotiate

@lesson 2.2 | Your plan B | 🅱️ | stage
hook: Someone offers $150 for your old bike. Should you take it? It depends on your plan B.
learn: Your plan B is what you'll do if this deal doesn't happen. Negotiators call it your BATNA: your best alternative to a negotiated agreement.
learn: A strong plan B gives you the confidence to say no.
learn: If another buyer has offered $180, you know $150 is too low.
play: path | You're selling your bike. A buyer offers $150. Another buyer said they'd pay $180 next week. | Politely say you have a higher offer and ask if they can match it => good: Your plan B gave you the confidence to ask. They might match it. | Accept $150 straight away => bad: You left $30 on the table. | Call $150 an insult and storm off => bad: You might lose a buyer for nothing. Calm and clear works better.
check: mcq | What's a BATNA? | Your best plan B if the deal falls through* | A kind of bat | A bank account
check: tf | A strong plan B makes it easier to say no to a bad deal. | true
check: mcq | You have no other buyers, so your plan B is weak. What could help? | Find more options before you negotiate* | Pretend you have one | Give up
home: Before your next negotiation, write down your plan B.
skill: negotiate

@lesson 2.3 | The first offer | 🎯 | stall
hook: You're selling a second-hand game. Should you name a price first, or wait for them?
learn: The first number said often sets the range for the whole talk. This is called anchoring.
learn: If you know what it's worth, a confident first offer can help. Research first.
learn: Aim a little high but stay reasonable, so there's room to meet in the middle.

check: tf | It's smart to research what something is worth before you name a price. | true
check: mcq | Similar items sell for $40. Which opening price is most sensible? | $45* | $150 | $5
home: Look up the second-hand price of something you own on a selling site.

@lesson 2.4 | Trade, don't cave | 🔄 | stall
hook: The buyer says "Can you do it cheaper?" Do you just drop your price?
learn: When you give something, ask for something back.
learn: Use "if… then": "If you pick it up today, then I can do $70."
learn: Small trades keep the deal fair for both sides.
play: chat | Someone wants your old keyboard | them: Would you take $60 instead of $80? | me: If you can pick it up today, I could do $70*/OK, $60 is fine/No way, $80 or nothing | them: Hmm. How about $65, and I'll come right now? | me: Deal. See you soon*/Make it $90/Actually, never mind
check: mcq | Which is a good trade? | If you pick it up today, I can do $70* | Fine, whatever you want | I'll pay you to take it
check: tf | When you give something in a deal, it's fair to ask for something back. | true
check: mcq | What does "if… then" help you do? | Trade instead of just giving in* | Make threats | Confuse people

@lesson 2.5 | Listen to negotiate | 👂 | stage
hook: The best negotiators often talk less. Why?
learn: Ask open questions, like "What matters most to you?"
learn: Sum up what you heard: "So the main thing is getting it by Friday?"
learn: Listening helps you find the deal, and makes people more willing to say yes.
play: sort | Helps the deal, Hurts the deal | What matters most to you?=Helps the deal; Interrupting=Hurts the deal; So you need it by Friday?=Helps the deal; Rolling your eyes=Hurts the deal; Tell me more about that=Helps the deal; Checking your phone=Hurts the deal | Does it help or hurt?
check: mcq | Which is an open question? | What matters most to you?* | Is it yes or no? | Are you done?
check: tf | Summing up what someone said shows you listened. | true
check: mcq | Why do good negotiators listen? | It helps find a deal both sides like* | It wastes time | So they can interrupt later
home: In your next conversation, sum up what the other person said before you reply.
skill: listening

@lesson 2.6 | Negotiating at home | 🏠 | home
hook: You want a later curfew. Your parents say no. Is it a fight or a negotiation?
learn: At home, it's a negotiation when both sides can get something they want.
learn: Find out their worries, like safety and sleep. Offer a plan that answers them.
learn: Suggest a trial: "Let's try 10:30 for a month. If I'm late once, it goes back to 10."
play: debate | Is offering a trial period a good way to negotiate a later curfew? | Yes. It answers their worries and lets you prove you're trustworthy* | No. You should just demand it | Some parents have firm rules for good reasons, and a trial may not change their mind. It's still a respectful way to ask.
check: mcq | A strong way to ask for a later curfew? | Offer a plan and a trial period* | Slam the door | Stay out late anyway
check: tf | Understanding your parents' worries helps you negotiate. | true
check: mcq | Your trial worked and the new curfew stuck. What helped most? | Keeping your side of the deal* | Luck | Nagging
home: Pick something to negotiate at home. Write down their worries and your plan.
skill: negotiate
