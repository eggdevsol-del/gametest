learn: STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result.
learn: Situation and Task set the scene briefly. Action is what you did, so spend most of your answer there.
learn: Result says how it turned out, with a number or a lesson if you can.
play: build | Build a STAR answer about a team problem | Situation: our group project was a week behind; Task: I had to help us finish on time; Action: I split the jobs and set mini-deadlines; Action: I checked in with everyone each lunchtime; Result: we handed it in a day early and got a merit
check: mcq | Which part of a STAR answer should be longest? | The Action* | The Situation | The Result
check: tf | A real example is stronger than saying "I'm a good problem-solver". | true
home: Write a STAR answer about a time you helped someone.

@lesson 6.3 | "Tell me about yourself" | 🙂 | workshop
hook: It's the first question in lots of interviews. What do they actually want to hear?
learn: They want a short, relevant summary, not your life story.
learn: Try present, past, future: what you're doing now, what you've done, and why you want this job.
learn: Keep it to about 30 to 60 seconds.
play: order | Put a "tell me about yourself" answer in order | I'm in Year 10 at Hillview College; I've volunteered at the food bank for a year, sorting and packing; I'm often trusted with the till at our school fair; I'd love this job because I enjoy helping customers
check: mcq | How long should this answer be? | About 30 to 60 seconds* | Ten minutes | Five words
check: tf | "Tell me about yourself" is a chance to share your whole life story. | false | Keep it short and linked to the job.
home: Practise a 45-second "tell me about yourself" answer with a grown-up timing you.

@lesson 7.4 | Not all feedback fits | 🧭 | workshop
hook: Five people give you five different opinions on your essay. Do you change it five times?

learn: Look for patterns. If three people say the ending is weak, it probably is.
learn: Weigh who it's from. The teacher marking it knows more about the task than a random commenter.
play: debate | Should you change your work every time someone gives you feedback? | No. Look for patterns, weigh who it's from, then decide* | Yes. Every comment must be followed | Sometimes one comment is spot on, even if nobody else said it. Weigh it on its merits.

check: tf | Feedback is information to weigh, not orders to follow. | true
home: Think of feedback you've had more than once. What's the pattern telling you?

@lesson 8.1 | The pause before you post | ⏸️ | online
hook: You're furious about a ref's call, and your thumb is hovering over "Post". Should you?
learn: Angry posts are quick to write and slow to undo.
learn: Before posting, ask: Is it true? Is it kind? Would I say it to their face? Would I be OK with a future boss seeing it?
learn: If you're not sure, save it as a draft and look again tomorrow.
play: path | Your team lost after a bad ref call. You've typed a post naming the ref and calling them blind and useless. | Save it as a draft and look again tomorrow => good: By tomorrow, you'll probably want to tone it down, or delete it. | Post it now, everyone's thinking it => bad: Naming and insulting someone can hurt them, and it can come back on you. | Post it, then delete it in an hour => bad: An hour is plenty of time for screenshots.
check: mcq | What's a good test before posting? | Would I be OK with a future boss seeing it?* | Will it get the most likes? | Is it the angriest thing I can say?
check: tf | Saving a post as a draft gives you time to cool down. | true
home: Next time you feel like posting something angry, wait until tomorrow and see if you still want to.
