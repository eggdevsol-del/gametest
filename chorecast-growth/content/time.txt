@lesson 3.6 | What changed after | 🔧 | townhall
hook: After each crisis, rules change. Did it work?
learn: After 2008, banks around the world had to hold more of their own capital and more cash, under rules called Basel III.
learn: Deposit protection, stress tests and rules on how loans are sold all grew stronger.
learn: New crises still happen, like crypto collapses, which is why rules keep evolving.

check: tf | New kinds of crises can still happen after rules change. | true
check: mcq | What does a stress test check? | How a bank would cope in a crash* | How stressed staff feel | How fast the website is

@lesson 4.3 | Winners and losers | ⚖️ | stall
hook: A strong dollar sounds good. Is it good for everyone?
learn: A strong dollar makes imports and overseas travel cheaper.
learn: But exporters earn fewer local dollars for each sale overseas, and tourism here gets pricier for visitors.
learn: A weak dollar does the opposite: good for exporters, harder for importers and travellers.

check: tf | A weak dollar helps exporters. | true

@lesson 4.5 | Floating, fixed and pegged | ⚓ | museum
hook: Some currencies move every second. Others barely move. Why?
learn: A floating currency's price is set by the market. {nz:The NZ dollar has floated since 1985.~au:The Aussie dollar has floated since 1983.}
learn: A pegged currency is held at a set rate by its central bank. The Hong Kong dollar has been pegged to the US dollar since 1983.
learn: Pegs give stability but can break if the central bank runs out of reserves to defend them.
play: match | Match each system to its description | Floating=The market sets the price; Pegged=Held close to another currency; Managed=Allowed to move, but the central bank steps in; Shared currency=Several countries use one money, like the euro
check: mcq | What sets a floating currency's price? | The market* | A fixed rule | The weather
check: tf | Pegs can break if a central bank runs out of reserves. | true
home: Pick a country with a grown-up and find out if its money floats or is pegged.

@lesson 4.6 | Locking in a rate | 🔒 | bank
hook: An exporter gets paid in US dollars in six months. What if our dollar jumps by then?
learn: Hedging means locking in an exchange rate now for a future payment.
learn: A forward contract agrees today's rate for a date later on. It removes surprises, good or bad.
learn: Big exporters, airlines and importers hedge so they can plan prices and wages.
play: path | Your business will be paid 100,000 USD in 6 months. | Lock in today's rate with a forward contract => good: You know exactly what you'll get and can plan. | Wait and hope the rate moves your way => ok: You might win, but you might lose a lot. | Spend the money now as if you'd already been paid => bad: You don't have it yet, and the amount isn't certain.
check: mcq | What does hedging do? | Locks in a rate to remove surprises* | Guarantees extra profit | Changes the currency's name
check: tf | Hedging removes good surprises as well as bad ones. | true
home: Ask a grown-up whether they'd rather know a price for sure or gamble on a better one.
